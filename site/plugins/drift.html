DRIFT

Tape-modelled delay and pitch-warping echo.

DRIFT models the parts of tape delay people actually miss: wow and flutter with correlated low-frequency drift, head-gap loss on repeats, and a record path that saturates before it distorts. Push the transport speed and the repeats bend with it, the way a machine does. Tempo-sync, free-run, and a freeze that holds the loop without re-recording into it.

Inside DRIFT

What a tape machine does that a buffer does not

Six behaviours we modelled because leaving them out is what makes digital delay sound like digital delay.

01 / RECORD PATH

Saturates before it distorts

Bias, pre-emphasis and tape compression sit ahead of the head, so the first thing that happens when you push the input is thickening, not fizz. Three tape formulations change the knee and the high-frequency headroom, not just the amount of dirt.

02 / WOW AND FLUTTER

Two speed errors that know about each other

Wow runs at 0.1 to 2.4 Hz, flutter at 6 to 18 Hz, and both are driven from one slowly wandering source so they move together instead of averaging into noise. A drift control scales the whole spread from a serviced machine to one that needs a belt.

03 / PITCH WARP

Move the transport, bend the repeats

Changing delay time retimes the buffer rather than crossfading it, so the tails in flight glide up or down and land at the new setting. Automate it in a straight line for a slow detune, or snap it for the sound of a hand on the reel.

04 / FEEDBACK TONE

A loop with its own filters

Head-gap loss darkens each pass on its own, and a 12 dB/oct high-pass and low-pass inside the loop let you decide where the repeats die. Past 100% the path self-oscillates and stays musical, because the saturation stage is inside the loop and not after it.

05 / DUCKING

Repeats that wait their turn

A program-dependent ducker pulls the wet path down while the source is playing and releases into the gaps, keyed from the input or from an external sidechain. Threshold and release are the only two controls, because the third one is always wrong.

06 / SYNC AND FREEZE

Locked, free, or held

Tempo-sync from 1/1 to 1/32 with dotted and triplet divisions, free-run in milliseconds to four seconds, and a freeze that holds the loop and stops recording into it — so you can play over the held bar without smearing it.

    Questions

    Before you put it on a send

    Is DRIFT a convolution of a real machine?

    No. A convolution captures one moment of one machine and then repeats it exactly, which is the opposite of what tape does. DRIFT is a running model: the record path, the head gap, the speed error and the saturation are all computed per sample, so no two repeats are identical and the tenth one has drifted a long way from the first.

    Can I automate the delay time without artefacts?

    You can automate it and get artefacts, on purpose. Delay-time changes retime the buffer instead of crossfading it, so tails in flight bend in pitch. If you want the clean behaviour, switch the transport to its stepped mode and the plugin crossfades between taps instead.

    Does it self-oscillate?

    Past 100% feedback, yes, and it stays usable because the saturation stage sits inside the loop. The loop high-pass and low-pass decide where the oscillation settles, so you can park it on a note rather than on a wall of low mid.

    What does the freeze control actually hold?

    The contents of the loop at the moment you press it, with the record path disabled. Wow, flutter and the loop filters keep running, so a frozen bar still moves — it just stops taking in anything new. Release it and recording resumes without a gap.

    Does it add latency?

    None. DRIFT reports zero latency in every host and every format. Turning on 2× or 4× oversampling for the record path does not change that — the oversampled section is inside the wet path, which is already delayed.

    Will my 1.0 sessions open in 1.1.0?

    Yes. Parameter IDs have not moved since 1.0, and the two controls added in 1.1.0 default to the values that reproduce 1.0 behaviour. What changed and why is written down in the changelog.
